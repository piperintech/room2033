    Spin School

    Lessons on how records and music actually work, then the practical basics for your turntable.

    Lessons

    Lesson 01

    How a record makes sound

    The groove is the sound wave itself, carved into plastic. It wiggles left and right and up and down. The stylus rides that wiggle, and tiny magnets in the cartridge turn the motion into a very small electrical signal. A phono preamp boosts that signal and corrects its tone (the "RIAA curve"), then the amplifier and speakers turn it back into air moving.

    Try this: with the volume all the way down, put your ear close to the needle while a record plays. You'll hear the music faintly, straight from the groove.
    Lesson 02

    The sound chain

    Stylus
    The diamond tip that touches the record. Replace it, not the whole cartridge, when it wears out.
    Cartridge
    Turns motion into signal. The biggest upgrade on the turntable itself.
    Preamp
    Built in, or a separate box. Required.
    Amplifier
    Powers passive speakers. Powered speakers have one inside.
    Speakers
    Change the sound more than anything else.
    Upgrade order: speakers first, then cartridge, then turntable. Expensive cables make almost no difference.
    Lesson 03

    Listen like an audiophile

    Soundstage
    How wide and deep the music feels, past the speakers.
    Imaging
    Whether you can point to where each instrument sits.
    Dynamics
    The jump from whisper to loud.
    Tonal balance
    Bass, mids and treble, none drowning the others.
    Detail
    Breaths, fingers on strings, the room.
    Try this: sit centered between your speakers, as far from each as they are from each other. Play "Money" on The Dark Side of the Moon and follow the cash register sounds moving across the room.
    Lesson 04

    The language of music

    Melody
    The tune you hum.
    Harmony
    The chords under it. Major feels bright, minor feels sad.
    Rhythm & tempo
    The pulse and its speed, in beats per minute.
    Timbre
    The color of a sound. Why a banjo and a piano playing the same note sound different.
    Arrangement
    Who plays what, and when they come in or drop out.
    Production
    How it was recorded and mixed: the room, the effects, the space.
    Try this: play Caamp's "Vagabond" and Mumford & Sons' "The Cave". Both use banjo. Listen to how differently it sits in each mix.
    Lesson 05

    Why vinyl sounds different

    Digital is technically more accurate. Vinyl's magic comes from other places: records are often mastered with less loudness squeezing, so quiet and loud parts breathe more. There's a little surface noise, and songs near the center of a side lose some detail because the groove moves slower there. That's why albums often put quieter songs last on a side.

    Try this: play the same song on vinyl and on your phone at matched volume. Notice which one makes you lean in, not which one is "better".
    Lesson 06

    Pressings and mastering

    First press
    The original batch. Collectible.
    Reissue
    Pressed again later, sometimes from the original tapes, sometimes from digital files.
    Remaster
    The sound was re-processed. Can be better or worse.
    AAA
    All analog, from tape to vinyl. Prized by purists.
    Half-speed
    Cut at half speed for precision. Common on audiophile editions like MoFi.
    Runout
    The smooth band near the label with etched codes. Type them into Discogs to identify your exact pressing.
    Try this: look in the runout groove of your Rolling Stones record and find it on Discogs.
    Lesson 07

    An album is a story

    On vinyl, the order matters. Artists choose what opens side A, what closes it, and what makes you get up and flip. Concept albums like In the Wee Small Hours and The Dark Side of the Moon are built to be heard in one sitting. Read the credits too: the producer and engineer shape the sound as much as the band.

    Try this: follow a producer. Ethan Johns produced Paolo Nutini's Sunny Side Up and Kings of Leon's early albums. Hear what they share.
    Lesson 08

    A short history of the record

    1948
    Columbia launches the 12-inch LP at 33⅓ rpm. Albums are born.
    1949
    RCA answers with the 7-inch single at 45 rpm.
    1982
    The CD arrives, and vinyl slowly fades.
    2008
    The first Record Store Day.
    2020
    Vinyl earns more than CDs in the US for the first time since the 1980s.
    Try this: Sinatra's In the Wee Small Hours (1955) was one of the first albums made for the LP format. Put it on and imagine hearing an "album" for the first time.
    Lesson 09

    Digging like a collector

    Light
    Tilt a used record under a light. Hairlines are fine; scratches you can feel with a fingernail will click.
    Warps
    Look along the edge at eye level. It should be flat.
    Sleeve
    Check for water damage and mildew smell.
    Price
    Scan the barcode with the Discogs app in the store to see what it really sells for.
    Clean
    Wash used finds before the first play to protect your stylus.
    Try this: spend 20 minutes in the $1–$5 bins at Half Price Books. The best finds are often there.

    The basics

    First setup (do this once)

    1. Find a level, solid spot. Not on top of a speaker. Vibrations and tilt make the needle skip. A cheap bubble level or a level app on your phone works.
    2. Remove the shipping bits. Take off the plastic stylus guard over the needle, any twist-tie on the tonearm, and the transport screw if the manual mentions one.
    3. Put on the platter and belt if they came separate. The manual shows how the belt loops around the motor pulley.
    4. Set the tracking force (only if your arm has a counterweight on the back). Balance the arm so it floats level, zero the dial, then turn to the grams your cartridge asks for, usually 1.5–2.5 g. Set anti-skate to the same number.
    5. Connect the sound. Turntables need a phono preamp. If yours has a PHONO/LINE switch, use LINE for powered speakers or a normal amp input, PHONO only if the amp has a "Phono" input. Wrong choice = super quiet or very loud and distorted.

    Playing a record

    1. Hold it by the edges and the label. Never touch the grooves; skin oil attracts dust.
    2. Pick the speed. Most albums are 33⅓. 7-inch singles and some audiophile pressings are 45. It's printed on the label or sleeve.
    3. Brush it. A few slow turns with a carbon-fiber brush while it spins.
    4. Cue it. Lift the cue lever, move the arm over the first groove at the outer edge, lower the lever. Let it drop slowly on its own.
    5. Turn the volume up after the needle lands, not before.
    6. At the end, lift with the cue lever, return the arm to its rest, and put the record back in its inner sleeve right away.

    Keep them alive

    • Store records standing up, like books. Stacked flat, they warp.
    • Keep them out of the sun and away from heat. A Texas car in summer will ruin a record in an hour.
    • Swap paper inner sleeves for anti-static poly-lined ones.
    • Replace the stylus roughly every 500–1,000 hours of play.
    • Close the dust cover while it plays if your deck has one.

    Starter kit (cheap, worth it)

    • Carbon-fiber record brush
    • Anti-static inner sleeves (pack of 50)
    • Outer plastic sleeves to protect the covers
    • Stylus brush
    • A cube shelf that fits 12.5-inch records (IKEA Kallax is the classic)

    Reading used-record grades

    MMint. Sealed or never played. Rare used.
    NMNear Mint. Looks and plays like new. Safe buy.
    VG+Light marks, plays clean. The sweet spot for price.
    VGSome surface noise and clicks. Fine for cheap finds.
    G / F / PNoisy, possibly skips. Skip unless it's rare.

    Where to hunt in Dallas

    • Josey Records (Dallas). Huge new and used stock.
    • Good Records on Lower Greenville.
    • Spinster Records in Oak Cliff.
    • Half Price Books. Check the used bins, all locations.
    • Record Store Day (April and Black Friday) for limited colored pressings.

    Online: Discogs (compare sellers, check feedback), eBay, the artist's official store, Target and Walmart sales.

    The ritual

    Vinyl is the opposite of shuffle. Pick one album, play side A start to finish, then get up and flip it. No phone. Lights low. The first time you hear an album this way, write one line about it in the record's notes in your crate, so the collection becomes a journal of what each record meant to you when you met it.
